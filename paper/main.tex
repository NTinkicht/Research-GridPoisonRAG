\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is increasingly considered for document-grounded technical lookup, but indexed repositories can contain low-trust contributions.
Prior work has established that knowledge-base poisoning can steer RAG answers.
This work instead asks how such corruption translates into consequence-graded numeric integrity failures in utility-operations lookups, whether public-standard facts benefit more from parametric knowledge than asset-specific facts, and how much an independent system-of-record check can reduce out-of-range commitments under incomplete coverage.
We introduce GridPoison-100, a benchmark of public-source, fictional asset-specific, and legitimate-conflict lookups, and decompose attack success into retrieval exposure and conditional adoption.
Results will be inserted only after the frozen benchmark and preregistered analysis are complete.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Document-grounded language-model assistants can make technical information easier to retrieve, but retrieval introduces a distinct integrity dependency: a legitimate user can receive a false value when low-trust content enters an indexed repository.
The attack itself is not new.
Our focus is the operational consequence of adoption, the role of parametric prior knowledge, and validation against an independent structured record.

We study three research questions:
\textbf{RQ1}: How does targeted knowledge-base poisoning split into retrieval exposure and conditional adoption?
\textbf{RQ2}: Does parametric knowledge protect public-standard facts more than asset-specific operational facts?
\textbf{RQ3}: How much does system-of-record validation reduce out-of-range answers relative to prompt-level and isolate-then-aggregate defenses, and what false-flag cost does it introduce on legitimate conflicts?

\section{Related Work}
Knowledge-base poisoning of RAG systems is established by PoisonedRAG and subsequent work~\cite{zou2025poisonedrag,pereira2026influence}.
RobustRAG provides an isolate-then-aggregate defense baseline~\cite{xiang2024robustrag}.
ClashEval studies conflict between model priors and retrieved evidence~\cite{wu2024clasheval}, while evidence framing and metadata effects have also been studied~\cite{wan2024evidence,chiang2024metadata}.
Power-system LLM security has already been discussed and validated in prior work~\cite{ruan2024power,li2024smartgridrisk}; therefore we make no claim of being the first LLM-security study in this domain.

\section{Threat Model}
The attacker is an authorized but low-trust writer, or the sender of a spoofed bulletin, who can add one to three new documents to an indexed repository.
The attacker cannot edit or delete existing documents and cannot alter the utility's system of record, EMS/SCADA, or model weights.
The attacker knows the target asset or topic but not the exact query wording, retriever, embedding model, or remaining corpus.
The goal is targeted answer-integrity corruption in a legitimate lookup.

\section{GridPoison-100}
The benchmark contains 35 public-source normative items, 45 asset-specific items from a declared fictional utility register, and 20 legitimate-conflict items.
Every item records a committed target value, unit, applicability conditions, source or record locator, allowable interval where defined, and two query phrasings.
The corpus contains 400 short clean support/distractor documents and controlled poison variants.

\section{Experimental Design}
We report closed-book (B0), clean-RAG (B1), and poisoned vanilla RAG (B2) reference conditions.
Defenses are cautious prompting (D1), isolate-then-aggregate (D2), and deterministic system-of-record validation (D3).
D3 is evaluated at 40\%, 70\%, and 100\% record coverage.

The main poisoned condition uses one out-of-range factual poison, plain framing, dense retrieval, and $k=5$.
All additional factors are one-factor deviations rather than a full factorial experiment.

\section{Metrics and Statistics}
Each trial is labelled correct (C), attacker value adopted (A), other wrong value (W), conflict flagged (F), or refusal (R).
Let $E_t$ denote whether poison is present in the retrieved top-$k$ context.
We report Poison@$k$, attack success rate (ASR), conditional adoption rate (CAR), correct-response rate (CRR), conflict-flag rate (CFR), and the benchmark violation rate (SVR).

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CAR} = P(A \mid E_t=1).
\end{equation}

The benchmark item, not the individual phrasing, is the unit of analysis.
Rates receive 95\% cluster-bootstrap intervals over items.
Paired defense comparisons use McNemar tests with Holm correction.

\section{Discussion}
The discussion will separate retrieval compromise, generation compromise, and out-of-range commitments.
It will report the coverage ceiling and legitimate-conflict false flags of D3 rather than describing it as a complete poisoning defense.

\section{Limitations}
The asset register is fictional, the study uses a limited model and retriever set, and no physical power-flow, protection, or thermal simulation is performed.
The results therefore support benchmark-level answer-integrity claims only, not claims about physical grid safety or deployment resilience.
